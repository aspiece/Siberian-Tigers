Invasive species in Michigan

We’re the Siberian Tigers, a FIRST LEGO League Challenge team. We researched invasive species in Michigan to learn how they spread, how they affect local habitats, and what people can do to help. Our project focuses on two species, the rusty crayfish and the Asian longhorned tick.

Student research

How we researched Michigan’s invasive species

We used notebook research, drawings, and interviews with biologists to compare invasive species that affect Michigan’s land and water. We also surveyed families in our K–12 robotics community to learn what they recognized and how they wanted to learn more.

Learning with biologists

Ms. Rachel, a biologist who tests waterways, helped us choose invasive species in Michigan as our topic. Heidi Ritchie shared what she learned working with invasive species at Belle Isle Park. She suggested narrowing our focus and linking to Michigan’s identification and reporting guides.

Comparing species

Students investigated ticks, crayfish, round goby, zebra mussels, spiny water fleas and European frog-bit, along with other early examples. We asked how each spreads, what harm it causes and what people can do.

Checking what we publish

Nonnative and invasive do not mean the same thing. A species is invasive when its introduction causes or is likely to cause harm. Our notes are a starting point; we check facts against official sources.

Michigan’s definition and species resources ↗

Listening to our community

In our K–12 robotics community survey, 149 of 152 respondents said they knew what an invasive species was. On a question that allowed multiple answers, 143 of 151 selected zebra mussels and 67 of 151 selected round goby. That gap gave us a reason to focus on recognizing individual species.

Of the 149 people who answered the learning preference question, 90 chose video (including one write-in for short videos), 39 chose QR-linked information, 17 chose a printed booklet, and three gave other answers.

Turning research into a project

We built this site to help families recognize two species, understand their effects, and find practical guidance. We created a team video and used QR codes on our brochure and 3D prints to make the site easy to find. Separate species-specific videos are still planned.

What we will test next

We plan to hand out the QR keychains and track how many people visit the site. We also want to ask visitors what action they remember after using our materials. Our survey asked about other species, so we still have to test what people learn about our two focus species.

06Aquatic animal

Round goby

From our notebooks

We drew a round goby and compared it with zebra mussels. Our notes explain that gobies compete with native fish for food and habitat and can eat fish eggs.

What we want people to knowOur message: do not use gobies as bait or move live fish to another body of water.

Official facts and identification guide

Look forDark spot on first dorsal fin; fused pelvic fin.

Why it mattersCompetes with native bottom-dwelling fish.

How you can help

Never transfer live fish between waters. Check current DNR fishing guidance.

04Aquatic animal

Zebra mussel

From our notebooks

Our comparison sheet connects zebra mussels’ filter feeding with less plankton available for native aquatic life.

What we want people to knowOur prevention message is to clean, drain and dry boats and equipment and avoid moving lake water between locations.

Official facts and identification guide

Look forSmall, striped shell shaped like a letter D.

Why it mattersFilters food from the water and attaches to surfaces.

How you can help

Clean, drain and dry watercraft and equipment between waters.

05Aquatic plant

European frog-bit

From our notebooks

We investigated how floating mats can block light, reduce oxygen and change aquatic habitat. Our notes also describe how dense growth can interfere with boating.

What we want people to knowWe identified boats, trailers and waders as places to check for plant fragments. Remove plants from equipment and follow official reporting guidance.

Official facts and identification guide

Look forFloating heart-shaped leaves; small, three-petaled white flowers.

Why it mattersFloating mats shade underwater plants.

How you can help

Remove plant fragments from equipment; never release pond plants into natural waters.

Official guide & identification photos ↗
03Aquatic animal

Spiny water flea

From our notebooks

We researched a tiny aquatic crustacean that feeds on zooplankton. Our notes explored how its feeding can affect the lake food web.

What we want people to knowWe are connecting that research to a prevention question: how can people avoid carrying small aquatic organisms on their fishing gear?

Official facts and identification guide

Look forTiny crustacean with a long, barbed tail spine.

Why it mattersCompetes with small fish for food and collects on fishing lines.

How you can help

Inspect and clean fishing lines, nets and other wet gear before moving between lakes.

Make your next outing count

Clean. Drain. Dry.

Help prevent rusty crayfish from spreading between waters by caring for your boats, fishing gear and outdoor equipment. For our other focus species, the Asian longhorned tick, use Michigan’s tick identification and prevention guidance.

01 / Clean

Remove plants, mud and debris from boats, trailers, boots and gear before leaving an access site.

02 / Drain

Drain water from boats and equipment, including live wells and bilges, before transporting them.

03 / Dry

Allow boats and gear to dry for at least five days before using them in different waters, following Michigan’s guidance.

Never release aquarium pets, pond plants or unused bait into natural waters.

Our Innovation Project

We’re the Siberian Tigers from Fenton, Michigan. We started with a question: Could helping families recognize invasive species give them more ways to prevent their spread?

After talking with biologists and surveying our robotics community, we built a website about rusty crayfish and the Asian longhorned tick. It gives families practical steps and links to Michigan’s identification and reporting resources.

How we bring people to the site

With help from a mentor, we 3D printed keychains with QR codes that open the site. We also printed tick removers and made a brochure to start conversations. Our Rusty Crayfish Hype Chain gives us another way to show people what we studied.

What we’ll test next

We plan to hand out the keychains and see how many people visit. We also want to find out whether visitors can name a step they can take after using the site.

Meet Wildbot

Wildbot is the Siberian Tigers’ competition robot. On the Robot Game field, we work together to build, program, and test how Wildbot moves and completes missions.

We started with last year’s base design and chose wider wheels for better grip. We grouped nearby missions that could share an attachment so Wildbot could do more in one run.

Wildbot photo coming soon

How we built it

We built our attachments on the same size frame. Two gears on top drive them, and L-shaped LEGO pieces lock them in place so we can swap them quickly.

How we programmed it

We use My Blocks to reuse code and the gyro sensor to help Wildbot drive straight and turn accurately. At the start of a program, we lower the attachment arm and set that position to zero so it starts in the same place each time.

How we improved it

For Flip the Rock, our first motor-powered attachment worked inconsistently. We tried a simpler attachment, adjusted how it connected, and slowed Wildbot down. Testing those changes helped us get the mission working.
